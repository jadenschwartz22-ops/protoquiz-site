Protocol census · a finding

Protocols change between editions.

An agency publishes a new edition of its protocol book, and what moved is not always easy to see. This page compares 140 dated books from 46 agencies in sequence and shows what appeared and what stopped appearing between them — each one quoted from the page it was found on.

Nebraska Board of Emergency Medical Services · NE

Chest tube / finger thoracostomy was not in the September 2022 book. It is in the May 2024 one.

Same agency, same statewide document, 5 dated editions of it. The May 2024 edition names it on page 144; the September 2022 edition does not name it anywhere.

Finger Thoracostomy only approved for Paramedic level if the following criteria are met: ... Tube Thoracostomy only approved for Paramedic level if the following criteria are met:

Nebraska Board of Emergency Medical Services, May 2024 edition, page 144

We did not infer this from a word count. The quote above was re-found on the page it was cited from before this event was allowed to publish, which is what “verified” means everywhere on this page. Confirmed by hand against the source PDF: the 2024 edition's page 144 is headed “THORACOSTOMY (Revised 5/7/2024)” — the agency dating its own change — and the term appears nowhere in the 2022 edition.

The fourth number is the one to read first. Comparing two PDFs produces mostly noise — a reorganised chapter, a term the extractor missed, one edition twice the length of the other — and 1,097 candidates were rejected to publish 110. A further 55 survived the guards but were never re-read against the page, so they are leads and are not shown here. 47 edition pairs came back with no difference at all, which is also a result.

Why a row says “stopped naming” and never “stopped doing”

This is the whole discipline of the page. When a procedure is in the earlier edition and not the later one, what we know is exactly that: the later book does not name it. We do not know the agency stopped doing it. A protocol book is written to guide care, not to enumerate a scope of practice, and a procedure can move to a separate policy, a state rule, a training bulletin or a medical director’s memo and go on being performed every day.

So no row on this page says an agency stopped doing anything, and if you find one, it is a bug. Both editions were read successfully — a book we could not parse produces no rows rather than an empty one, because an unread document would otherwise look like an agency that dropped everything.

What we deliberately do not publish

We can tell you that 5 authorization changes and 7 certification changes were detected between editions, and we will not draw them as a trend. They were measured and they did not survive: the readings pointed both ways in similar numbers and none of them was grounded well enough to publish. The extractor is stable comparing one region against another and is not stable comparing one agency against its own past, and until that is fixed a direction drawn from these would be a picture of our instrument rather than of EMS.

The half that does hold — who decides, compared across regions at one moment — is on the authorization page.

How far this reaches

11 agencies have a published change here (AL, IL, MA, MD, MI, NE, OH, PA, VA), out of roughly 26,883 licensed EMS agencies nationally. This is not a national picture of how EMS is changing and cannot be read as one: an agency is here only if it publishes dated editions, keeps them readable, and published at least two we could hold side by side. What it is good for is showing that the changes are real, specific, and findable — and that nobody is currently telling the people who work there.
