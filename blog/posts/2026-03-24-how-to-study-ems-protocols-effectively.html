How to Study EMS Protocols: A Practical Guide for Medics

By Jaden • March 24, 2026 • 4 min read

You read the protocol book, feel confident, then freeze on a call trying to recall a dose, a contraindication, or which pathway fits a patient who does not match one algorithm. That is not a lack of effort. Most of us just study protocols in ways that feel productive and do not stick.

Why Re-Reading the PDF Fails

Protocols are written to be looked up, not memorized. And re-reading, even with a highlighter, builds familiarity, not recall. Recognizing Adenosine 6mg on a page is not the same as pulling that dose from memory while managing a conscious patient in SVT.

Active Recall

Close the book and ask yourself: what is the dose, route and concentration of Epi in cardiac arrest? You will get things wrong. That is the point. The effort of retrieval is what builds retention.

  • Self-quiz: read a section, close it, write down what you remember, check it.
  • Flashcards: drug on one side, dose, route, indications and contraindications on the other.
  • Protocol quizzes: tools that generate questions from your protocols cover the whole book without hand-making hundreds of cards.
  • Teach-back: explain a protocol to a partner without the book. If you cannot, you do not know it yet.

Spaced Repetition

Ebbinghaus showed in the 1880s that we forget on a predictable curve, losing most new material within 24-48 hours without review. Spaced repetition schedules each review just before you would forget: a day, then three days, a week, a month. Items you miss come back sooner. The SM-2 algorithm does this automatically, and ProtoQuiz's Learn Mode applies it to your agency's medications. More in spaced repetition for EMS providers.

Scenarios

Facts are not enough on a call. Scenarios make you combine protocols (chest pain can mean cardiac, pharmacology and transport at once), decide with incomplete information, sequence interventions, and find gaps flashcards miss. Practicing with a partner or instructor limits how often you can do it. AI-generated scenarios built on your protocols let you run them on your own time with immediate feedback.

A Weekly Routine

  • Daily, 10-15 min: spaced repetition review of what is due. Miss a day? Just resume; do not double up.
  • 2-3 times a week, 15-20 min: one scenario or algorithm quiz, rotating categories, then re-read the protocol for anything you missed.
  • Weekly, 20-30 min: read one section you have not touched lately, focusing on why the guideline says what it says.

Roughly 2-3 hours a week, and far more effective than a 4-hour cram once a month.

Common Mistakes

  • Only studying before recert. A little consistently beats a lot sporadically.
  • Using generic NREMT prep for local protocols. If your doses or drugs differ, it can reinforce the wrong answer.
  • Skipping the algorithms. Knowing what to give matters less if you do not know when.
  • Studying drugs in a vacuum. Knowing why Amiodarone is second-line in your protocol matters more than its dose alone.
