Med Mastery Stopped Marking You Wrong on Drugs You Know

By Jaden • September 17, 2026

The old Med Mastery could mark you wrong on a drug you know cold. You typed the right dose for the right indication, said nothing about the second indication, and the whole answer came back red. That is the worst thing a study tool can do to you, so this update rebuilt how the typed answers are graded.

It is rolling out now. Update to 2.6.2 when it shows up on the App Store and you will have all of it.

The Question Now Asks for the Whole Fact

Hard mode used to ask for a drug's doses and grade you against a flat run of numbers glued onto one line. That is not how the fact lives in your protocol. A dose without its indication is not a fact at all: the same drug carries a small dose for one presentation and a much larger one for another, and the bare numbers give you no way to tell which was which.

The question now asks for indications, doses and routes together, and the expected answer prints one line per indication with the indication labelled. When a session is scoped to adults or to pediatrics, the stem says so, so an adult answer is never graded against a pediatric key.

Two smaller shapes changed with it. Contraindications are asked in the plural even when your protocol lists one, because a singular question quietly tells you how many to look for. Drug class is only asked when your document actually states one, so you are never asked for a fact the answer key does not hold.

Partial Credit, With Rules

Before, there were two verdicts: right, or missed. Now a multi-part answer can come back partial, and partial credits the parts you got right while leaving exactly the parts you missed still due. Name three of four indications and the fourth is what comes back around, not the three you already own.

Where it gets strict is numbers. Wording is graded generously, because clinical shorthand is still correct: an abbreviation, a synonym, a different spelling of the same term all count. A dose does not work that way. The number and the route have to match your protocol. Half the correct dose is not partially right, it is wrong, and crediting it would teach you that a wrong number is nearly good enough.

Partial credit also has to earn its way into your progress. If the grader comes back partial but cannot say precisely which lines you missed, nothing moves in either direction. The alternative is treating everything not explicitly flagged as a miss as something you knew, which turns a vague answer into free progress and eventually calls a drug mastered that you never demonstrated.

Underneath all of it sits one rule: when the grader genuinely cannot tell, you get the point and both answers are shown side by side. A false wrong teaches a medic to distrust a correct memory, and that is worse than not having the feature.

What Changed in Med Mastery

One question for indications, doses and routes, with the patient named when the session is age-scoped

One line per indication in the expected answer, instead of a run of numbers with no labels

Partial credit that re-drills only what you missed

Exact on numbers and on routes, generous on wording

The Formulary first, before your first session

You Pick the Meds Before You Start

The Formulary has been in the app a while and almost nobody found it. It is every medication pulled out of your protocol, with a switch on each one, and it decides what Med Mastery drills you on. Never open it and you get drilled on the whole document, including drugs your service does not stock.

So it opens by itself now, once, before your first Med Mastery session, with a line explaining what it is. Turn off what you do not carry, and continue. It is not a gate: continue, close, or swipe it away and the session you asked for starts either way. Whoever dismissed it has seen the Formulary, which was the whole point. It returns one more time if you reset your Med Mastery progress, since a reset is the other moment your deck is worth a second look. The Study screen card is called Formulary now, and says what it does.

Your First Week Is Full Premium

New accounts get seven days of everything, with no payment method and nothing to cancel. It is granted when you finish setting up the app, whether or not you ever opened the subscription screen, and it expires on its own. No card is involved, so there is no charge at the end and nothing to remember to turn off.

After the seven days you land on the free tier, which is still there and still free: three of each generated feature a day.

A full week is the only honest way to answer whether this app is worth paying for. Screenshots do not answer it. A restricted version does not either, because what you are actually evaluating is whether drilling your own protocol on your own schedule sticks, and you cannot feel that in one session.

Everything Else in This One

  • Algorithm quizzes weight toward your certification level instead of sitting at the level everyone shares, so a paramedic gets drilled on paramedic decisions with fundamentals still in the mix, and nothing above your scope.
  • Scenarios and algorithm quizzes hold their layout. The question no longer shifts down the screen when you submit an answer.
  • The loading bar is paced to how long generation actually takes instead of a fixed guess, so the number you are watching matches the work.
  • The feedback form keeps your draft when you dismiss the keyboard, which it had been quietly throwing away.

The Honest Part

Grading typed free-text against your own document is a hard problem and this is not the last version of it. These are the rules I could defend, and they fail toward giving you the point rather than taking it away. If it still marks you wrong on something you know, the feedback form in the app is the place to say so. It will keep what you typed now.

And the standing rule: your local protocol always wins. ProtoQuiz drills you on your own document. It is not medical direction, and it never overrides your agency and your medical director.
